- question: Do interactive cat toys actually stop boredom-related behaviour?
  answer: They help, but only if they genuinely engage a cat's hunting instinct rather than just making noise. A toy that triggers stalking, pouncing and "catching" does far more for an under-stimulated indoor cat than one that just sits there — which is why unpredictable movement tends to outperform toys with fixed, repetitive motion.
- question: Are electronic cat toys safe to leave running unsupervised?
  answer: Most are designed for some unsupervised play, but it's worth checking the specific toy — some have automatic shut-off timers precisely so they don't run indefinitely or overheat, and small parts like feathers or detachable motors are best removed if your cat is a determined chewer rather than just a batter.
- question: What's better for a bored indoor cat — a wand toy or a self-play toy?
  answer: They solve different problems. A wand toy needs you there to drive the motion, which makes it the better choice for bonding and controlled exercise, while a self-play toy (a track, a treat dispenser, a motion-sensor toy) fills the gaps when you're out or busy. Most multi-cat or heavily indoor households end up with at least one of each.
- question: Why does my cat ignore an interactive toy after a few days?
  answer: Novelty wears off fast with cats, especially toys with fully predictable motion. Rotating 2-3 toys in and out of use, rather than leaving everything out permanently, tends to keep interest going much longer than buying one "best" toy and expecting it to hold attention indefinitely.

A toy that gets batted once and then ignored isn't doing much for a cat that spends most of its day indoors. Here's what actually keeps cats engaged, and which toys are worth buying.

## What actually matters

- **Unpredictable motion beats repetitive motion** — a toy that moves the same way every time stops triggering a hunting response quickly; irregular speed or direction changes hold interest far longer.
- **Decide if it needs you there or not** — wand toys only work with a person driving them, while tracks, treat dispensers and motion-sensor toys can run solo, which matters if your cat is home alone for long stretches.
- **Battery life and charging method** — USB-rechargeable toys avoid the ongoing cost of AA or AAA batteries, but check how long a charge actually lasts under daily use before buying.
- **Noise level** — some electronic toys have a noticeable motor hum, which can be a dealbreaker in a small flat or if the toy's meant to run while you're working from home.
- **Size and destructibility** — plush toys with detachable motors or stuffing are easier to keep clean and replace parts for, which matters more for cats that treat toys as prey to be dismantled rather than just chased.
