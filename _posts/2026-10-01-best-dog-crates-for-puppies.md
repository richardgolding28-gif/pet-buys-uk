- question: Is crate training actually kind to a puppy?
  answer: Done properly, yes — dogs are naturally den animals, and a crate gives a puppy a space that's reliably theirs, which can reduce anxiety rather than cause it. The key is never using it as punishment, building up time in it gradually, and making sure it's genuinely big enough that it never feels like confinement.
- question: What size crate does a puppy actually need?
  answer: Big enough to stand up, turn around, and lie down fully stretched out, but not so big they can toilet in one corner and sleep in another. This is exactly why divider panels matter — buy for the adult size and use the divider to shrink the usable space while they're small, rather than buying a crate that's immediately too small.
- question: How long can a puppy realistically be left in a crate?
  answer: As a rough guide, a puppy can typically hold their bladder for about one hour per month of age, up to a maximum of around 4 hours for an adult. A young puppy shouldn't be crated for a full working day, crate or not.
- question: Wire crate or plastic/soft crate for a puppy at home?
  answer: Wire crates are generally better for home use since they offer better ventilation and visibility, which tends to suit puppies that are still getting used to being left alone. Soft-sided and hard plastic crates come into their own for car travel and vet visits rather than as a permanent home setup, and soft fabric ones are easier for a teething puppy to chew through.

A crate that's the wrong size, wobbles when your puppy leans on it, or needs replacing within six months isn't actually saving you money. Here's what to look for, and which crates are worth buying.

## What actually matters

- **A divider panel included** — this is the single biggest cost-saver, letting you buy one crate sized for your dog's adult weight and shrink the usable space down while they're small.
- **A genuinely secure latch** — puppies that work out how to nudge a simple slide bolt open will keep doing it; look for double-lock or slide-bolt-with-safety-catch designs.
- **A removable, washable tray** — accidents are part of crate training a young puppy, and a tray that lifts out for cleaning makes this far less unpleasant than one that doesn't.
- **Enough ventilation and visibility** — wire crates generally suit a puppy settling into being left alone better than solid-sided ones, since they can still see and hear what's going on.
- **Rounded edges and no exposed sharp wire ends**, especially on the base, since puppies spend a lot of time pawing and chewing at the crate itself before they settle.
